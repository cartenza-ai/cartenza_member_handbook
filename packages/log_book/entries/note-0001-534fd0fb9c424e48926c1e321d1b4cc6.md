## Level 1 — The Log Book: Don't Lose What Already Happened

**The constraint this solves:** conversations and context get forgotten. AI-assisted work produces real reasoning, decisions, diagnostics, and observations — but that knowledge is usually trapped inside one chat session or model. Starting a new session often means re-explaining everything from scratch, and the human ends up as the bottleneck: the one who has to stop and reconstruct what already happened.

**Cartenza's response:** a persistent log book. Capture what happened, when, and why — outside the chat itself, in git. That makes the record portable, versioned, inspectable, and independent of whichever AI provider or session produced it.

**What this looks like in practice, in this system:** `knowledge_repo`'s `add_log_entry`/`append_log_entry` tools. Four entry types — `observation`, `decision`, `lesson_learned`, `routine_def` — plus freer note/milestone/issue framing layered on top. Every entry lives as its own file, in git, in a named package. See `getting_started`/"Which MCP Server Do I Use?" if you haven't already — this is the layer `knowledge_repo` owns.

**Why this is the foundation, not just one feature among several:** every layer above this one — Knowledge Packages, Routines, Playbooks, Projects — depends on there being a durable, queryable record underneath it. Nothing above this staircase level works if the raw material (what actually happened, and why) isn't captured somewhere real in the first place.

**Why the entry stays simple, on purpose:** everything built on top of the log book — Knowledge Packages, Routines, Playbooks, Projects, and structural things like Purpose statements and Release Trains — was built *on top of* the same simple entry shape, not by making the entry itself more complex. That is not an accident, and it is worth noticing as a design choice rather than a limitation.

It echoes a decision Agile methodologies made decades ago: XP, Scrum and Kanban all settled on index cards, specifically because a card's small size forces brevity and its physical form stays easy to move, discard, and rewrite. An entry that stays simple stays malleable. Complexity belongs in what gets built on top, not in the base unit itself.

The practical consequence for you: **resist the urge to make an entry carry structure it doesn't need.** If something feels like it wants a schema, that is usually a signal it belongs at a higher level — a package convention, a type configuration, a routine — not a heavier entry.

**A concrete signal you're working at this level, and that's fine:** you're logging observations and decisions as you go, one entry at a time, without yet needing to search across many entries or repeat a workflow. That's the normal, healthy starting point — not a sign you're behind.

**Reference:** Cartenza's internal design history (the original staircase reasoning this section is drawn from, and the first-hand observation about entry simplicity that this section records), `getting_started` package (which server owns this).

### Attaching things to an entry: point, don't carry

An entry holds **text**. It does not hold files, and that follows directly from the simplicity above rather than being a missing feature — the moment an entry carries attachments, it stops being a card you can move, rewrite and discard.

So when the thing you want on the record isn't text — a photo of a test rig, a scan, a vendor PDF, a slide, a screenshot of something outside a model — **put it where your team already keeps files and link to it from the entry.** Google Drive and SharePoint are both good answers, and for most organizations they are the right one:

- **Your sharing rules keep applying.** Access stays governed by the system your company already administers, rather than by who happens to hold a link. For anything with a customer name or a supplier's drawing on it, that is not a convenience — it is the requirement.
- **The file stays where people look for it.** One copy, in the place it belongs, rather than a second copy that quietly diverges from the first.
- **The entry stays small.** Which is the whole point of the entry.

**Write a sentence next to the link.** What it shows, and why it mattered. A bare URL in an entry is a dead end six months later, when nobody remembers what they were meant to notice in the picture — and the sentence is the part that still works even if the link does not.

**A link is only as durable as what it points at.** A file moved between folders, or re-shared from somebody's personal drive, breaks the trail silently. Prefer a stable shared location over a personal one, and prefer the folder your team will still be using next year.

**One case works differently.** Something Cartenza itself rendered — a diagram drawn from a Capella model, say — can be preserved as an artifact in your own repository and cited directly, with no external storage involved. That path carries provenance a file in a drive cannot: it records the version of the model it came from. See the `mcp_tools` package for how that works, and use it in preference to a screenshot whenever the content came from a model.

**Reference:** `mcp_tools` (preserving a rendered view), and the entry-simplicity reasoning above, which this section is an application of rather than an exception to.
